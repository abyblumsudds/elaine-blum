Why a Neurodiverse-Affirming Approach Matters

By Elaine Blum

There are a lot of therapeutic approaches out there that claim to be “client lead” and “strengths based” yet get a lot of things wrong about neurodiversity. These are the approaches that too quickly criticize overthinking and intellectualizing – even though these are often hallmark strengths of neurodivergent brains. These approaches champion evidence based practice yet do not in good faith acknowledge how these approaches were developed within clinical trials that centered white, cis-male experiences.

When one group makes rules and regulations that constrain opportunities and care for another group without the latter’s input, this is a form of oppression. We can call it neuronormativity, or assumptions about what a normal brain should be able to do that classify certain neurotypes as fully capable and others as at a deficit.

I have had experiences as both client and colleague of therapeutic practitioners that relied on deficit models of neurodivergence. I have heard that evidence based practice tells me that this or that strategy should lessen my depression. That I am ruminating or that I need to learn how to better name and sit with my feelings or that I need to “check the facts” to alleviate excessive negative self-talk. I have been told by practitioners and colleagues alike that intellectualizing is a problem, that it avoids attunement with emotions. I had clinicians lecture me on black and white thinking while simultaneously endorsing forced dichotomies between intellect and emotion, fact and feeling. Once enough practitioners go to the same well and you still don’t feel better, you start to think that the problem must be you. That somehow you are not implementing the tools correctly, you’re not trying hard enough, that you are the problem.

False. The problem is with applying approaches built for neurotypical brains within a context where those tools cannot gain traction. Your brain does not need fixed. All brains have both strengths and weaknesses, overabundance and scarcity. If a tool or an approach has not worked for you, then that is not the right approach.

Neurodiversity names ways in which brains may diverge from the presumed norm, including ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder), autism (ASD), OCD (obsessive compulsive disorder), dissociative identity disorder (DID), and other neurotypes. In my practice, I specialize with folx who have autism and ADHD; I have worked with folx who experience all of the above.

Through specializing in my study and practice with neurodiverse folx, here are some myths that I am working on deconstructing for myself and others:

Myth: Intellectualizing is (always) bad.

Yes, intellectualizing can be a defense mechanism, a way to avoid engaging one’s emotions (I can help with that, too!). However, a lot of neurodivergent folx have strengths in intellect, in being able to see connections and patterns and make creative inferences before others can (or do). The danger in this overgeneralization – that all intellectualizing is bad – is that you send signals to your neurodiverse clients that the way that our brains work, the way that we engage and process emotions, is something to be fixed. This deepens the impulse to second guess ourselves, to work against how our brains want to work. This can be at best draining and at worst harmful to the neurodiverse brain.

Myth: I overthink things.

Is this something that you’ve been told when you have asked for further detail or clarity? Or is it that you experience thoughts that are on a loop, going nowhere? Overthinking suggests that there is a normative or “right” level of thinking. Yet clarity and consistency cue safety and assist with emotional regulation. For many neurodivergent folx, we internalize the message that we overthink things, coded as “The level of detail that your brain needs to feel safe is not ok to need and not ok to ask for.” But what if some folx simply think at a higher register, a higher level of complexity? We all go different speeds.

Myth: Internal validation is the goal.

This assumes an ableist, one-size-fits-all approach. Neurodivergent brains thrive on external and social supports. Sometimes we need that external cheerleader helping amplify the parts of ourselves that we see as our best. This helps offset the myriad sociocultural signals – both implicit and explicit – that teach us to rely on external validation. Early education is dominated by behavioral approaches that teach us to expect rewards for good behavior and punishment when we do it wrong. Neurodiverse brains are also taught from an early age that our needs – because they diverge from the expected, the norm – cannot possibly be right. So, we learn to distrust that inner voice and rely on external confirmation. Unlearning this self-distrust is hard!

What if some people more effortlessly “don’t care” about what others think because their environments/situations are set up to more automatically/unreflectively meet their needs? For example, what if our world is structured such that it automatically satisfies the needs of those who have power to shape it? Neurodiverse brains thrive with external supports. Let’s find ways to amplify the preferred voices that tune into the parts of yourself for which you desire recognition.

Myth: (All) Neurodiverse folx struggle with black-and-white thinking.

Counterpoint: A lot of neurodiverse folx have strengths in seeing patterns, connections, and nuance. What others see as black-and-white thinking may be a learned and/or trauma response, an attempt to develop rules to systematize what appears to be an overwhelmingly rich and chaotic world. Or, it might be an attempt to translate neurodivergent experience – with its wealth of connections – into perceivably more simplified neuro-normy language. It also could be that we are attempting to convey a strength of feeling by appealing to more absolutist language than we would otherwise endorse. Regardless of neurotype, we all have non-negotiables, things that appear as given, and things toward which we get, shall we say, persnickety. A neurodiverse-affirming approach finds footholds and bridges within your own worldview, language, and capacities.

Myth: (All) Neurodiverse folx struggle with naming their emotions.

Some neurodiverse folx feel deeply and with a range that exceeds neat classification within an emotions wheel. Yes, alexithymia (difficulty understanding, identifying, and/or expressing emotions) can be part of a neurodiverse experience. If it is part of yours, we can work on strategies to build emotive intelligence! On the other hand, it might well be the case that you do already engage your emotions, maybe in a way that sounds or looks different than others. Perhaps in a way that expresses emotions through a more “intellectualized” lens. Sometimes hesitancy toward expressing emotion can come from habitually triaging what can be ignored or de-prioritized within an overwhelming world. Or, it could be in part from being taught to dampen or ignore big emotions, through messages like “Don’t be a drama queen.” A neurodiverse-affirming approach acknowledges that emotional expression has a wide range and can look a lot of different ways.
